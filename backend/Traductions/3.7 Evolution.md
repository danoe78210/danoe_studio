I approach a campfire. About ten Homo sapiens have gathered around the flames. They have been hunting all day. Now they share the cooked meat. Their communication consists of articulated sounds, gestures, facial expressions. An elder—gray hair, wrinkled face—tells a story. The others listen. He gestures broadly, mimics an animal, recounts its adventures. The youngsters watch, spellbound. They learn.

"Oral transmission of knowledge."

It is no longer instinct. It is culture. A collective memory that words and gestures perpetuate.

A woman rocks a child. She sings softly—a simple, repetitive, soothing melody. The child falls asleep against her.

"Love. Care. Protection."

Humans watch over their young with remarkable devotion. They protect them, feed them, educate them. Each generation passes its knowledge to the next.

"It's beautiful."

A young adult adds wood to the fire. The flames rise, illuminating faces. They draw closer, seeking warmth.

"The fire."

Like dragons, they master fire. But differently. Dragons exhale it, a product of their biology. Humans capture it, domesticate it, bend it to their needs. They transform nature.

"It's remarkable."

I rise higher. Soon the whole continent spreads out beneath me: savannas, forests, deserts, mountains. Everywhere, scattered, small campfires. Humans. Thousands of humans. Tens of thousands.

"You are many… And you multiply. You disperse. You migrate. You adapt. You are the future of this planet."

The dragons have chosen isolation. They will remain in their mountains, silent witnesses. But you, humans, will conquer the world.

"And I will be with you, but I will not control you."

It is important. You must evolve freely, make your own choices, commit your own mistakes.

"You must become yourselves."

Millennia pass.

I stay. I watch. I protect discreetly. Time flows differently for me now. Each human moment could be infinitesimal, each generation a heartbeat. Yet I choose to match their rhythm. I want to see every instant, every progress, every spark of genius.

"I must understand them to guide them better."

More than anything, I must be sure I have made the right choice—that these fragile creatures justify the sacrifice of my essence.

What I see fascinates me.

*400 000 years before our era*

I fly over a northern European plain. The sun is sinking on the horizon. The air is fresh, almost cold. A group of Homo heidelbergensis—about ten individuals—moves silently through the tall grass. I descend, invisible, to observe more closely.

"A hunt."

They are tracking a herd of wild horses. The animals graze peacefully near a shallow lake, unaware of danger.

"Coordination."

The humans split up. Some circle the herd on the left, others on the right. Two stay back, hidden in the grass. This is not instinct—it is strategy. They have surveyed the terrain, identified a spot where the horses cannot escape easily: the lake on one side, a steep slope on the other. One of them—a robust man with long hair—holds something in his hand. I move closer to see.

"A spear."

Not a simple pointed branch. A carefully carved wooden thrusting spear, balanced, its tip hardened in fire. Two metres long, perfectly straight. Suddenly, a signal. I do not perceive it clearly—perhaps a gesture, perhaps a brief sound. But all react at once. Those who were hidden rise abruptly, shout, wave their arms. The horses startle, panic, gallop. Exactly as planned, they flee toward the lake. There, other humans surge from both sides and encircle them. A younger, slower horse becomes isolated. The humans converge on it. The first throws his spear. The projectile arcs through the air, spinning slightly, and strikes the horse on the flank. The animal whinnies, stumbles. A second spear follows. Then a third. The horse collapses. The humans rush forward. One of them—perhaps the leader—approaches cautiously, a sharp stone in hand. He checks that the animal is dead, then lifts the stone above his head. The others shout—a cry of victory, joy, relief. They gather around the carcass and begin to butcher it. Their movements are precise, efficient. An older woman supervises. She shows the younger ones how to cut, what to keep, what to discard. They work together. Some slice the meat; others prepare hides to carry the pieces. A youth gathers the long bones, rich in marrow.

"Tools. Cooperation. Transmission of knowledge."

Three foundations of their future greatness.

"You are progressing."

*200 000 years before our era*

Here I am beside a lake. The sun is high in the sky. A Homo sapiens sits on a flat rock. In front of him are flint shards, stone hammers, fragments of wood.

I approach, invisible.

He grasps a flint piece in his left hand. With his right, he strikes it with a rounded hammer stone. Each blow follows intention. He does not act at random. The final tool already exists in his mind. He knows its shape. He anticipates each percussion.

"Abstraction. Imagination. Creativity."

He continues. Slowly, the flint takes shape. A blade emerges—long, thin, sharp, perfectly symmetrical.

"Beauty."

The tool exceeds mere utility. The blade already cuts, but the craftsman persists, seeking symmetry, harmony. When finished, he examines it, rolls it between his fingers, tests the edge cautiously. A smile appears.

He then takes a prepared wooden handle. A notch cuts the end. He slides the blade into it. Tendons bind the assembly, tightened firmly. Resin seals everything. Several materials. Several techniques. Several successive gestures. The whole becomes a composite tool. The man does not limit himself to what exists. He fashions what was not.

"Transformation. Creation."

I stay until he rises and walks away, tool in hand, ready to hunt.

*100 000 years before our era*

A cave. Dusk is falling. A group of Homo sapiens is gathered inside. Silent. Solemn. In the centre lies a body—an elderly man, dead.

"They honor their dead."

It is not the first time I have witnessed this. But this time it is different—more elaborate, more ritualised. They have laid the body carefully: fetal position, hands clasped, face turned eastward—toward the rising sun.

"Symbolism."

Around the body they place objects. Tools: a flint blade, a scraper. Food: dried meat, fruit. Flowers: I recognise yarrow and centaury. Their petals are wilted yet still coloured.

"Offerings."

Why? The dead no longer needs anything. He can no longer eat, use tools, or smell flowers.

"They believe in something beyond death."

That is the only explanation. They think the dead continues to exist, that he needs these objects in another world. A woman—perhaps his partner—kneels beside the body. She weeps. Tears run down her cheeks. She touches the dead man's face one last time. She murmurs something—words I do not yet understand, but whose emotion I feel.

"Sorrow. Love. Loss."

The others gather around her. They touch her, comfort her. They share her pain.

"Empathy."

A man—perhaps the deceased's son— speaks a few words. The others listen, heads bowed. A prayer, perhaps, or a tribute, or a recounting of a life.

"Ritual."

Together they cover the body with earth. Slowly, with respect. Then they mark the spot with stones arranged in a circle.

"Memory."

They do not want to forget. They mark the place to return, to remember.

I am moved.

"These fragile beings, who live barely a few dozen years, grasp something profound."

Suddenly a thought crosses my mind.

"And if they honored me too?"

Not directly. They do not know me. They do not see me. Yet they honour what transcends them, what rises above their fleeting existence.

"They give meaning to the inexplicable."

Death is unfair, final, sometimes absurd. Yet they turn it into something beautiful—into ritual, into collective memory.

"That is exactly what I do."

I, too, fight entropy. Against oblivion. I preserve. I protect. I transmit.

"Somewhere, we are alike; we refuse to let things disappear without a trace."

I watch the grieving woman. Her sorrow is immense. Yet within that sorrow there is love. Within that love, memory. Within that memory, the dead continues to exist.

"That is what I have been seeking."

What I see goes beyond intelligence or curiosity. It is a capacity to transform suffering into beauty, to refuse oblivion.

"You are my heirs."

I remain, invisible, until they leave the cave, allowing the dead to rest in peace.

*50 000 years before our era*

Night has fallen. A campfire burns. A family of Homo sapiens is gathered around the blaze. They have just eaten. Now it is time to listen to the elder.

I step closer, curious.

Their language has evolved. It is now more complex, richer. What I hear are words, sentences, real grammatical structures. The old man tells a legend.

— Long ago, very long ago, before our ancestors walked this earth…

He evokes a time when immense beasts ruled these lands, fierce and terrible.

— One day, a blinding light tore the sky and consumed everything.

He speaks of the comet. It happened millions of years ago. Yet the memory persists, passed down generation after generation—distorted, mythologised, but present.

— Almost everyone perished. Our ancestors found refuge in caves. But they were not alone. In distant mountains, winged creatures survived. Tall as trees, they breathe fire. At night, it is said they can be seen hunting. Some believe they protect us; others think we are their prey. One thing is certain: they guard treasures, fragments of stones that fell from the sky during the cataclysm.

Thus they know dragons and their secret, although almost no living human has ever seen one. Collective memory defies time, carried by stories.

— And they are not the only ones watching over us. There are also invisible spirits that exist to guide us during hunts, during storms. We do not see them, but they are there. Always.

"Me."

They speak of me. Unaware that I truly exist. They feel my presence. They perceive my influence. And they have turned it into myth.

I am shaken.

They know me without knowing me. They honour me without knowing who I am. They invoke imagined worlds, myths that give meaning to their existence.

The storyteller finishes his tale. The whole family lets out satisfied cries. The children’s eyes are wide, fascinated.

"They turn the past into stories."

And those stories bind them. They create a collective identity. They give purpose to their place in the world.

"You create myths."

*40 000 years before our era*

In a deep cave, torches illuminate the walls. A couple of Homo sapiens are busy decorating the walls with drawings. One prepares pigments, the other paints.

"Art. Primitive, but art nonetheless."

Different pigments are mixed: red ochre, black charcoal, white clay. The artist uses his hands, his fingers, also sticks and sponge pads to trace shapes. He draws a lion. The feline appears to leap. Its paws are in motion. Its mane flows. There is perspective, depth.

Why spend hours in a dark cave, far from camp, painting animals on the walls?

"To express."

To leave a trace. To tell the world, "We exist. We feel. We create."

The artist continues. He paints other animals: aurochs, mammoths. Each is unique. Each has personality. Then he does something that strikes me. He presses his hand against the rock, takes a hollow bone, fills his mouth with pigment, then splashes the liquid around his outstretched fingers. When he lifts his hand, a white silhouette stands out against the red background.

"This is his signature. I was here. I existed."

How can he imagine that, thousands of years later, children will discover his work by accident?

*Recurring, across the millennia*

A scene repeats through ages and territories. A child wanders from the group. He sits on a rock and watches the star‑filled night. He picks up a shiny stone, strikes it against another. Sparks fly. He laughs. He does not make a tool. He does not start a fire. He plays, imagines, dreams.

"Pure curiosity."

That is what I have been waiting for. This curiosity. This need to understand. This way of asking “why?” without seeking immediate utility.

— Dear child.

I address him in silence. He perceives nothing distinct. Yet something passes. A breath. A gentleness.

— Within you lies the promise of the future. Keep questioning, keep dreaming. That is what will make you great.

The child looks up at the sky. He smiles.

— You are destined for an extraordinary fate, I am now certain of it.

Millennia flow.

Homo habilis fashions rudimentary tools. Homo erectus masters fire. Homo neanderthalensis buries its dead with respect. Homo sapiens paints on walls, invents gods, creates musical instruments, watches the stars, wonders about the origin of the world.

They evolve, move, explore new territories—from Africa to Europe, Asia, Australia, then the Americas. They adapt, invent, build.

*30 000 years before our era*

I stand on a hill. The night is clear. No clouds. Not far from me, two young hominids have their heads tilted toward the sky. They point out constellations. They talk.

— Look! Those lights form a hunter.

— Yes, and there, a bear!

— There are so many. What could they be?

— Maybe fires, like ours, but far away.

— Do you think there are beings like us living on each of them?

— Why not?

— Do you think one day we could go there?

Both laugh.

— First we’d have to fly like birds!

— Still, I’m convinced there is something out there!

I am moved. They wonder what lies beyond. They dream of touching the infinite.

"You have just taken a step toward me!"

They are touching, trying to count the stars, to grasp colors, shapes…

"The infinite calls you… as you resemble me! Keep going. Do not disappoint me!"

Sometimes I sense the Stranger’s shadow hovering in the distance. If he no longer intervenes, he surely watches my little hominids. His voice echoes in my mind, distant, mocking.

— You made a strange choice with these creatures. It piques my curiosity…

— Your curiosity… or your fear? I am sure their unpredictable evolution escapes your comprehension!

No answer.

He watches closely, waiting for a weakness, an opportunity to discredit my work or prove existence futile, even to claim consciousness is merely illusion.

"But I will not give him that satisfaction!"

At last, I hope to meet him on equal terms.

Having observed all these moments, all these evolutions, all these advances, I have come to a deep conviction.

"This species is destined for an extraordinary fate."

They are fragile, yes. They make mistakes, yes. They are sometimes violent, cruel, selfish. Yet they are also curious, creative, resilient. They turn tragedy into art. They turn fear into myth. They turn death into respect.

"They are not perfect."

But that is precisely why they need me.

"And perhaps, one day, I will need them."
